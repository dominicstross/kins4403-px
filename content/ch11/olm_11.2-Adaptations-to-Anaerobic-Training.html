Part 1: Changes in Anaerobic Power & Laboratory Testing

▶ Concept Reading: Evaluating Anaerobic Capacity and the Wingate Paradigm

Assessing Anaerobic Power and Capacity

In muscular activities that require near-maximal force production for relatively short periods of time, such as sprinting, jumping, or Olympic lifting, energy needs are met predominantly by the ATP-phosphocreatine (PCr) system and the anaerobic breakdown of muscle glycogen (fast glycolysis). Evaluating adaptations to anaerobic training presents unique experimental challenges. Unlike aerobic power, for which maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max) serves as the universally accepted gold standard, exercise scientists have had difficulty agreeing on an exclusive laboratory or field test that captures total anaerobic power.

The Three Established Assessment Paradigms

Most exercise science research evaluating anaerobic performance relies on three established testing protocols:

  • The Wingate Anaerobic Test (WAnT): A 30-second all-out cycle ergometer protocol against high braking resistance. It is the most widely utilized and standardized anaerobic test in human performance laboratories.
  • The Critical Power Test: Mathematical modeling of power output across multiple exhaustive trials to determine the critical power asymptote and anaerobic work capacity (W').
  • The Maximal Accumulated Oxygen Deficit (MAOD) Test: The difference between theoretical supramaximal oxygen demand and actual oxygen consumption during exhaustive exercise.

Methodology of the Wingate Anaerobic Test

During the Wingate anaerobic test, the subject pedals a cycle ergometer at maximal velocity for 30 seconds against a high fixed braking force. The braking resistance load is precisely determined based on the individual's body mass (typically 0.075 to 0.085 kg per kg of body weight), sex, age, and level of conditioning. Power output is continuously recorded and averaged over 3- to 5-second sampling intervals throughout the 30-second effort.

Wingate Performance Metric Measurement Window Physiological Significance & Primary Bioenergetic System
Peak Power Output (PPO) Highest power achieved (typically first 5–10 s) Index of anaerobic power; driven by peak rate of ATP hydrolysis and rapid phosphocreatine breakdown via creatine kinase.
Mean Power Output (MPO) Average power across the entire 30 s period Index of anaerobic capacity; reflects sustained energy production combining the ATP-PCr system and fast anaerobic glycolysis.
Total Work Mean Power (Watts) × 30 seconds Index of cumulative anaerobic work capacity (expressed in Joules or kilojoules) over the test duration.
Fatigue Index Percent drop from Peak Power to Minimum Power Measures the rate of power decline and susceptibility to fatigue caused by PCr depletion and intracellular acidosis.

Training Adaptations in Anaerobic Power

Chronic anaerobic training, such as sprint running on the track or sprint interval training on a cycle ergometer, increases both peak anaerobic power and anaerobic capacity. However, training responsiveness varies widely across empirical research: studies demonstrate gains ranging from minimal or negligible improvements up to approximately 25%. These adaptations reflect neuromuscular recruitment, increased force development, and enhanced metabolic buffering rather than cardiovascular adjustments.

Part 2: Muscle Fiber Plasticity & Bioenergetic Enzyme Adaptations

▶ Concept Reading: Fiber Hypertrophy, Phenotypic Shifts, and the Costill Paradox

Skeletal Muscle Fiber Plasticity with Anaerobic Training

Anaerobic training, including high-intensity sprint training and resistance exercise, induces muscular changes that reflect motor unit recruitment for near-maximal force development. Per Henneman's Size Principle, at high workloads, Type II fast-twitch motor units are recruited to a much greater extent than during aerobic training, although Type I slow-twitch fibers continue to be recruited additively.

Hypertrophy and Phenotypic Transitions

  • Selective Hypertrophy: Both Type IIa and Type IIx muscle fibers undergo substantial increases in cross-sectional area (CSA). Type I fibers also increase in CSA, but to a lesser extent.
  • Sprint-Induced Fiber Shift: Chronic all-out sprint training (e.g., repeated 15- to 30-second maximal sprints) elicits a phenotypic shift: a reduction in the percentage of Type I fibers with a corresponding increase in Type II fibers, particularly Type IIa. Studies demonstrate Type I percentages decreasing from 57% to 48%, with Type IIa fibers increasing from 32% to 38%.
  • Comparison with Resistance Training: This Type I to Type IIa phenotypic shift is unique to high-velocity sprint training and is typically not observed in standard heavy resistance training, where shifts occur primarily within the fast-twitch pool (Type IIx converting toward Type IIa).

Adaptations in the ATP-PCr Phosphagen System

Maximal muscular efforts lasting less than 6 seconds place primary demands on the breakdown and resynthesis of ATP and phosphocreatine (PCr). In a landmark study by Costill and coworkers, participants trained one leg using repeated 6-second maximal knee extension bouts (preferentially stressing the ATP-PCr system) and the other leg using repeated 30-second maximal bouts (preferentially stressing anaerobic glycolysis).

The Costill et al. Enzyme Paradox

Costill and colleagues observed striking enzymatic divergence:

  • The activities of the key phosphagen enzymes creatine kinase and myokinase (adenylate kinase) increased significantly after repeated 30-second training bouts, but remained virtually unchanged in the leg trained with 6-second bouts.
  • Both 6-second and 30-second training bouts elicited the exact same muscular strength gain (~14%) and the exact same resistance to fatigue during a 60-second sprint performance test.
  • Conclusion: Sprint training bouts lasting ≤6 seconds develop muscular strength rather than expanding the enzymatic capacity for ATP and PCr turnover. Strength gains allow the athlete to perform an absolute workload at a lower relative effort, reducing fatigue.

Adaptations in the Anaerobic Glycolytic System

Repeated 30-second anaerobic training bouts increase the maximal activities of several key regulatory glycolytic enzymes by 10% to 25%, including glycogen phosphorylase, phosphofructokinase (PFK), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). In all-out sprint protocols, hexokinase activity has been shown to increase by up to 56% and PFK by 49%, while 6-second sprint bouts produce minimal change in these enzymes.

Creatine Kinase (CK) ATP-PCr Phosphagen Catalyzes transfer of phosphate from PCr to ADP to form ATP Elevated (+10% to +25%)
Myokinase (Adenylate Kinase) ATP-PCr Phosphagen Transfers phosphate between two ADPs: 2 ADP ↔ ATP + AMP Elevated (+10% to +25%)

Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) Glycolysis Interconverts pyruvate and lactate, regenerating NAD+ Elevated (+10% to +25%)
Succinate Dehydrogenase (SDH) Mitochondrial Respiration Krebs cycle enzyme transferring electrons into ETC Unchanged (No Effect)

The Specificity of Training (SAID Principle)

The cellular adaptations to exercise training adhere strictly to the principle of training specificity. Anaerobic training upregulates ATP-PCr and glycolytic enzyme activities but has no effect on mitochondrial oxidative enzymes. Conversely, aerobic endurance training increases oxidative enzymes (such as SDH and citrate synthase) without altering phosphagen or glycolytic enzyme capacity.
